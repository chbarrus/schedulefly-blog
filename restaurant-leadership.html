The humility of 89%

When Wil was 10, a camp coach told his team to give 89% — not 100%, not 110%. The lesson stuck for 28 years.

"Giving 100% all of the time is not possible. It sounds good, but it just isn't realistic. If you commit to 89% and stick to it, that's a more realistic goal — one you should feel very able to commit to."

The team went on to win more games than anyone in camp. The coach's second lesson was humility: they never told anyone their record, and they let other teams brag.

"We'll win them with class, and we won't brag. That's for people who don't expect to win."

Joe Johnston on building institutions

Joe Johnston built Joe's Real BBQ, Liberty Market, and Joe's Farm Grill — restaurants meant to last generations. A few of his principles:

Freedom comes from interdependence

"There's the idea that the American dream is independence — the self-made man who can do it alone. The fact is, nobody has the whole package. I decided I'm only going to do what I'm good at, and never do anything without partners who cover the operating side."

Take time to perfect your first creation

"The first creation — in your mind, on paper, in your business plan — is always better than the second creation. If you haven't spent time richly enjoying and embellishing it, the second creation is going to be half-cocked."

Restaurants don't have to be risky

"Restaurants are traditionally thought of as risky ventures. I don't necessarily consider them risky at all — if you do them correctly."

Meherwan Irani on vision and scarcity

Meherwan Irani built Chai Pani in Asheville, N.C. into five concepts. He studied why restaurants fail, and found one factor eclipsed all others.

"Lack of capital, bad food, bad location — all of those are eclipsed by the single, most overriding factor: lack of a defined concept that is easy to articulate. If somebody asks what kind of restaurant it is, you need to answer as simply and quickly as you can."

He also credits his success to an unlikely advantage:

"The critical factor was that our limited resources made us really think outside the box. We opened with two hundred and fifty dollars in the cash register and used social media instead of ads. We couldn't afford to advertise at all — yet anytime a charity asked, we'd jump all over it."

Why the goal wasn't wealth

Van Nolintha of Bida Manda in Raleigh — one of the most celebrated restaurants in North Carolina — explained why his team delivers such consistent hospitality.

"Our goal wasn't to be wealthy; our goal wasn't to create the best restaurant in Raleigh. Our goal was to make sure we share our narrative, food, and culture with our community the best we know how. For us, hospitality is hosting and welcoming people into our home."

His hiring philosophy is just as distinctive:

"What we're looking for is someone who's capable of caring. We have artists, makers, a yoga instructor, med students, law students. 'Intention' is the big word in our training process — and it's hard to train someone who doesn't already carry that sense of pride."

There is no secret

After years of interviewing successful owners, the clearest lesson is that there's no single formula — and that's liberating, not discouraging.

"The path to success is opaque. Each person's path, and each business's path, is different. The only secret formula is the one you figure out for yourself — and the only way to construct it is through trial and error."
